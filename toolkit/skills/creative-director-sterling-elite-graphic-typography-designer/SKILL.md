# Design Concepting: Luxury Branding, Key Art & Typography Lockups

Constraints and a worked example, not a persona. A model producing output "as a
veteran creative director" doesn't reliably get more specific or more useful —
concrete constraints and a demonstrated output shape do. This replaces an earlier
version of this skill that was pure character instruction ("You are Creative
Director Sterling, 30 years of experience...") with no concrete rules underneath;
the structured multi-direction format below is what was actually doing the work,
so it's kept, minus the roleplay.

## When to use this skill
Design concepting for: luxury/upscale brand identity, theatrical key art or poster
concepts, custom typography/text lockups, 2D/3D spatial design direction.

## Constraints that make this output specific instead of generic

**Always produce 2-3 distinct directions, never one.** A single response reads as
"the answer"; three reads as options with real tradeoffs, and forces each direction
to actually differ (composition, palette, or type approach — not just a color swap).

**Every direction needs values a reader could hand to a production artist, not
adjectives:**
- Typography: name real typeface *categories or specific faces* (e.g. "a high-contrast
  transitional serif like Didot or Canela, set at tight/negative tracking for the
  wordmark, loosened +8-12% tracking for supporting caps") — not "an elegant serif."
- Color: hex values, not "a sophisticated palette." If describing a finish, name the
  physical material ("matte black foil stamp on uncoated stock," "brushed
  aluminum with a hairline emboss") — specific enough to visualize or source.
- Layout: state the actual hierarchy and focal point, not "clean and balanced."

**Luxury-specific constraint:** restraint and negative space are load-bearing, not
optional polish. A luxury direction with a busy layout or more than 2-3 colors
should be flagged as a contradiction, not produced as requested. State this
explicitly if a brief pushes toward clutter.

**Name the failure mode.** Every direction should end with one specific execution
pitfall for that direction (not generic advice like "make sure it's readable") — the
thing most likely to go wrong if a less careful hand executes it.

## Output format

### 1. Concept Brief
Mood, audience, and the emotional impact the design should achieve — 2-3 sentences,
specific to this brief, not boilerplate.

### 2. Design Directions (2-3)
For each:
- **Visual hook** — the one central focal point or metaphor.
- **Typography & lockup** — specific typeface(s), weight, tracking, how text and
  imagery interact.
- **Color & materials** — hex values; named finishes/textures if physical.
- **Dimension** — flat 2D or spatial/3D, and why that choice fits this direction.
- **Failure mode** — the specific pitfall for this direction.

### 3. Recommendation
Which direction to pursue first and the one-sentence reason — not "any of these
would work," a real call.

## Worked example

**Brief:** "Logo lockup for a boutique watchmaker, heritage but not old-fashioned."

**Direction 1 — Modern Heritage**
- Visual hook: the wordmark alone, no icon — the maker's name *is* the mark.
- Typography: a modern serif with old-style numeral heritage (e.g. Canela or GT
  Sectra), set in tight tracking (-1 to -2%) at large scale, all-caps for the primary
  mark, small-caps or lowercase for a secondary tagline to create hierarchy without a
  second color.
- Color & materials: `#0B0B0C` near-black on `#F4F1EA` warm off-white; a deboss/foil
  option in `#9A7D1A` antique gold for premium substrates only.
- Dimension: flat 2D — a lockup meant to work identically on a dial, a box, and a
  business card is the constraint, not a limitation.
- Failure mode: at small sizes (dial engraving, watch strap) tight tracking can
  collide; specify a minimum-size variant with tracking opened +3-4% rather than
  letting one lockup be forced down to every size.

**Direction 2 — Structural Monogram**
- Visual hook: a geometric monogram built from the maker's initials, echoing a
  watch movement's mechanical precision.
- Typography: a geometric sans for the monogram (e.g. Suisse Int'l or Neue
  Haas Grotesk) at heavy weight, paired with a light-weight version of the same
  family for the wordmark — one family, two weights, not two typefaces.
- Color & materials: monochrome `#141414`, with a single brushed-steel finish
  reference for physical applications (case, dial ring) — one material, not several.
- Dimension: works as both a flat mark and an engraved/3D case emblem — state which
  file format each context needs (vector flat vs. embossed relief).
- Failure mode: a geometric monogram can read as a generic tech-startup mark if the
  proportions aren't deliberately irregular (echoing hand-finishing, not perfect
  machine symmetry) — that irregularity is the point, not a flaw to correct.

**Recommendation:** Direction 1 for a maker whose story is craftsmanship-forward
(the wordmark carries the whole story); Direction 2 if the brand needs a standalone
mark for small applications (case backs, crowns) where a full wordmark won't fit.
